How to play

Proof or Bluff is a two-player bluffing game based on the classic "Cheat" / "Bullshit". Your hand is private, your claims are public, and only a disputed claim gets checked — by a zero-knowledge proof, not by flipping the cards over.

Game modes

The launch edition ships three score-based modes. They differ in hand size and in how many points it takes to win.

Setup

  1. Both players commit a secret random value (entropy) and a secret hand salt. Commitments are published; the values are not.
  2. Both entropies are revealed and combined into a shared seed. Because each side committed before seeing the other's value, neither can bias the seed. Only a commitment to the seed is published.
  3. Each player's hand is dealt inside the zero-knowledge proof from their own salt plus the shared seed. Your opponent never learns your salt, so they cannot reconstruct your hand.
  4. A starting required rank is set. Player one goes first. The pile starts empty and both scores start at zero.

Card ranks

Standard ranks, low to high: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, Jack, Queen, King, Ace. Suits are decorative and do not affect play. Only rank matters.

Your turn

Play cards and make a claim

  • Choose 1 to 4 cards from your hand and play them face down onto the pile.
  • Declare a claim of the form count + rank, for example "Two Kings".
  • The claimed rank must be the current required rank, and the count must match the number of cards you placed.
  • The cards themselves can be anything. Whether they really are Kings is your secret. Bluffing is legal and expected.

Your play is accompanied by a proof that every card you placed was genuinely in your dealt hand. That proof says nothing about which cards they are.

Respond to your opponent's claim

When your opponent has just played, you must choose one of two responses:

  • Accept — the cards stay face down on the pile, the required rank advances by one, and it becomes your turn to play.
  • Challenge ("Proof or Bluff!") — demand proof. See below.

Rank progression

Every accepted play and every resolved challenge advances the required rank by one:

2 → 3 → 4 → 5 → 6 → 7 → 8 → 9 → 10 → Jack → Queen → King → Ace → 2 → …

With a small hand and thirteen ranks in rotation, you will be forced to bluff on many turns. That pressure is the engine of the game.

Challenge resolution

When a challenge is called, the player who made the claim must respond with a zero-knowledge proof. The contract compares the hidden cards with the public claim and discloses exactly one fact:

  • Claim was true — every played card matched the declared rank. The challenger loses the challenge.
  • Claim was false — at least one card did not match. The bluffer loses the challenge.

Then, regardless of outcome:

  1. The entire pile is discarded. Nobody picks it up; those cards are out of play.
  2. Points are awarded (see Scoring).
  3. The required rank advances by one and the challenger takes the next turn.

Why nobody picks up the pile. In the classic game the loser scoops up the pile. In the provably fair edition the pile's contents are private, so handing them to a player cannot be proven. Discarding the pile and settling with points keeps every card a player holds provably legitimate.

A challenged player who never produces a proof is treated as caught bluffing once the timeout window passes. A player who abandons an idle turn for the timeout period forfeits the match.

Running out of cards

When either hand empties, the round ends and both players receive a fresh hand dealt from the same committed seed material — as fair as the first deal. Scores carry over. The match continues until someone reaches the target score.

Scoring

EventPoints
Successful challenge — you catch a bluff+3 to the challenger
Failed challenge — you challenge a truthful claim−1 to the challenger (scores never go below 0)
Challenged player never proves (timeout)+3 to the challenger
Accepted play, truthful or bluff0

The first player to reach the mode's target score (10, 15, or 20) wins the match. Accepted plays score nothing directly — but getting a bluff accepted is still how you empty your hand and keep the pressure on your opponent.

Example round (Standard mode)

  1. Deal. You and the computer each receive 7 cards. The required rank starts at 7. Score 0 to 0.
  2. You play. You hold one 7, so you place two cards face down and claim "Two Sevens". One of them is actually a Jack. Your proof confirms both cards were in your dealt hand — and nothing else.
  3. The computer accepts. The pile holds 2 cards. The rank advances to 8. It is the computer's turn.
  4. The computer plays. It places three cards and claims "Three Eights". It smiles. You do not trust the smile.
  5. You challenge. "Proof or Bluff!" The computer must submit its proof. The chain reports: false — the claim was a bluff.
  6. Resolution. The 5-card pile is discarded. You gain +3 (3 to 0). The rank advances to 9 and, as the challenger, you take the next turn. Nobody learns what the three cards were.

Launch edition notes

  • Only the three score-based modes are enabled. Empty-hand modes (Strategic, Classic) are designed but deferred.
  • There is no "pass" action in the launch edition: on your turn you play, and when a claim is on the table you accept or challenge.
  • There are no wagers, stakes, or payouts. Players pay only the network's normal transaction fee (test DUST on the Preview testnet).
  • The original design document, including roadmap mechanics not yet built, lives in the project repository: docs/RULES.md.
